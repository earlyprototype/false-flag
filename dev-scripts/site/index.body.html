FALSE FLAG — Operation Tuman, an LLM-driven COBRA crisis simulation

{{mast}}
{{fog_thin}}

You are the Prime Minister of the United Kingdom. Russia has staged an attack on its own naval base and blamed Britain for it. Fifteen submarines are in the North Atlantic. Five advisors are around the COBRA table waiting for you — and not one of them is a menu.

FALSE FLAG is a crisis wargame that runs in a terminal. Every advisor, ambassador and head of government is an LLM agent with its own brief, its own memory of the campaign so far, and — in one mode — its own secret. You do not pick option (b). You write a paragraph in your own words, and the game adjudicates it: interprets what you actually ordered, lets the room object, and makes the world answer.

Read this first. Two different things are on this site and it matters which one you are looking at. The play page is the game itself: the same Python engine, compiled to WebAssembly and running inside your browser, with no server behind it. You type a paragraph and the room answers you. Everything else here is a record — real terminal captures from real runs, rendered to HTML with the colour and box-drawing intact. The campaign you can read is a genuine one, not a mock-up, but it is a recording: you are watching a game that was already played. The game's home is still a terminal, and the full build is the one you clone and run there.

02[ YOU WRITE A PARAGRAPH ]

This is the whole interface. There is no list of actions, no approval meter, no cooldown. The prompt is >: and it accepts English. Here is the first decision of the campaign on the replay page, exactly as it was typed —

{{TERM_DECISION}}

— and here is what the engine made of it, one LLM pass later: a reading of what was actually ordered, which forces it named, and how long it takes.

{{TERM_ORDER}}

Nothing in that order was on a menu. "I want the room itself watched — a record of who pushes which conclusion at what hour" is not a game verb. It became one, because the adjudicator is a language model and the world state it updates is written in the same language you gave it.

03[ THE ROOM ARGUES BACK ]

Before anything happens, your advisors read the decision and file objections. Militarily implausible, legally exposed, diplomatically reckless, domestically dangerous — each in their own voice, each with a specific recommendation attached.

{{TERM_ADVISORY}}

You then choose: apply all of it, apply some of it, rewrite the decision yourself, or ignore the room entirely and proceed. Ignoring five critical advisories is allowed. It is simply a thing you did, and the campaign remembers it.

AND YOU CAN PICK UP THE PHONE

/call russia opens a live conversation with an actor who is not on your side and does not share your brief. The ambassador knows things you do not; the transcript of your COBRA deliberations is filtered out of their context before they answer, so they can only respond to what you have actually said to them.

04[ THE HIDDEN NARRATIVE ]

This is the part that makes FALSE FLAG unusual. At campaign start you can choose Mystery Mode, and the game draws a secret truth — the actual shape of the crisis — and tells nobody.

{{TERM_MYSTERY}}

That truth is then injected into every foreign agent's system prompt as a motive they must act on and must not state. Russia may be exactly what it looks like. Or Russia may be somebody else's instrument, and the country whose ambassador keeps counselling calm may be the one running the operation. You are not scored on guessing. You just start noticing that Washington is holding intelligence back, or that Paris keeps taking meetings it does not mention, and you have to decide what to do about a suspicion you cannot prove.

[ SEALED ] Open the envelope — how a hidden narrative is actually written

Each narrative is a small YAML document giving every nation a secret motive, a public posture, and an intelligence-sharing level that ranges from Full to Sabotaged. This is one of them. If you would rather deduce it in play, close this.

{{TERM_NARRATIVE}}

The player never sees this file's contents in game. The advisors do not see it either — only the foreign actors do, each getting the global truth plus their own stance, which is why the room can be sincerely wrong.

07[ RUN IT YOURSELF ]

It is a Python project with six dependencies and no account to create.

LINUX / MACOS · SHELL 
git clone https://github.com/earlyprototype/false-flag.git
cd false-flag
python3 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/pip install -r requirements.txt
.venv/bin/python -m cli.main play

With nothing configured it runs in a deterministic mock advisor mode: you get the full turn structure — briefing, cabinet Q&A, decision, adjudication — with canned advisor voices and no network calls. That proves the game runs. It does not reason about what you wrote.

For the real thing, point it at any OpenAI-compatible endpoint by copying config.example.py to config.py and uncommenting one preset: Groq, OpenRouter, or a local Ollama model that needs no key at all. Every capture on this site was produced that way. Windows instructions, the provider comparison and the local-model walkthrough are in the README.

What this is not. It is not a product and it is not a forecast. The browser build is the real engine rather than a demo of one, but it is the same game brought to where you are, not a lighter version of it — and the replays elsewhere on this site stay recordings, not something you are playing. What FALSE FLAG is, is a wargame about how a small number of people make irreversible decisions on partial information, and it is honest about being a simulation with a language model in the middle of it.
