How a turn reaches the model — and the six bugs on that path

Every diagram here is drawn from the code as it now stands on claude/false-flag-game-ux-2j8rea. The bug write-ups say what broke, what a player would have seen, and what changed.

A single turn sends about fifteen requests to the model. Until this branch, every one of them went out alone, and none of them shared a single cacheable byte with any other. Fixing that meant giving a dormant code path its first callers — which is how most of the bugs below were found.

01What one turn actually sends

The game does not make one call and print the answer. A turn is a pipeline: an event is generated, advisors are asked, the player commits, the decision is interpreted, challenged, audited for omissions, adjudicated, and reacted to. Most of those calls do not read each other's answers — which is the whole opportunity.

02Where a call goes after it leaves the game

Every call site hands its prompt to the router. The router picks a provider, builds or reuses a driver, applies the rate limit, and sends. What matters most in this picture is the bottom edge: when a live call fails, the router answers from the offline mock driver instead of raising. The game keeps playing. Nothing on screen looks wrong.

03The bug that could put words in an advisor's mouth

Critical · fixed in 5fc5adf

A failed call spoke as the Foreign Secretary

What happened

When several calls go out together, the driver runs them in a thread pool. If one of them fails, it cannot raise — that would kill the whole group — so it catches the error and puts the string [ERROR: connection timed out] in that request's slot. The group returns normally. Nothing raises anywhere.

Why it mattered

The router's retry-and-fall-back-to-mock safety net only covers a request that raises. This one never does. The error string travels back as if it were the model's answer, and it is a perfectly ordinary non-empty string. The code that turns an answer into an advisor's spoken line does not recognise it as anything special, so it gets quoted. The player would have seen a cabinet minister stand up in COBRA and say [ERROR: connection timed out].

The fix

Reject the marker where an answer becomes speech, and fall through to the written fallback line instead. The actor-simulation path already guarded against this; the guard had simply never been carried across to the advisor path.

04The four bugs that surfaced when the parallel path woke up

The code for sending a group of calls at once existed in both live drivers and had no callers anywhere in the game. It had therefore never been exercised. Giving it its first callers exposed three defects immediately, and a property check found a fourth.

Major

The rate limit was silently skipped when calls went out together

What happened. The rate limiter sits in the router. The driver fans the group out across its own thread pool and never consults it.

Why it mattered. A configured requests-per-minute limit was simply ignored the moment a group was sent — five requests would leave at once regardless of the setting. Harmless while nothing called the function; live the instant something did. On a free tier that means refused requests, and a refused request comes back as the mock driver's answer.

The fix. The router claims one slot per prompt before dispatching, including on the retry — which is the dispatch most likely to breach the limit, since the first failure is usually the limit itself. The group still goes out together whenever the limit is not actually binding.

Major

The parallel path could not cap its output length

What happened. The group-sending function had no way to pass a maximum output length to the driver.

Why it mattered. Advisor reaction lines are capped at 150 tokens precisely because a model that is not told to stop will keep going until it exhausts its budget and then return nothing at all. That group is one of the three that should run in parallel — but it could not have both the cap and the parallelism.

The fix. The cap is threaded through, and only forwarded to drivers whose signature accepts it, so the offline and mock drivers are not handed an argument they never declared.

Major

Five advisors were auditing the turn nearly blind

What happened. The "did the Prime Minister fail to do something critical?" checks were reading 100 lines of history. Every other call read 500.

Why it mattered. 100 lines is roughly one turn. Five advisors judging whether the PM had failed to consult NATO could not see the turn in which they did — so the audit could fire a warning that the transcript directly contradicts.

The fix. One renderer now builds the history for every call, so this class of mismatch cannot recur. It also happens to be what makes caching possible; see below.

Moderate

The history builder could run past its own limit

What happened. When the history is too long to send whole, the builder keeps the campaign's opening and cuts the middle. It grew the opening one whole turn at a time, but took the first turn unconditionally.

Why it mattered. A transcript whose first turn marker sits a long way in pulled all of that preamble along regardless of the limit — 10,410 characters past the cap on the worst generated case. The limit exists to keep prompts inside the model's context window, so overshooting risks a rejected request, which again returns the mock's answer.

The fix. The limit is checked on every path out of the function, not just the one that trims. An earlier version of my fix covered only one of the three exits and was caught in review — a transcript could still reach 796,465 characters while sitting inside the line count.

05Why nothing could be cached, and what changed

Providers cache from the beginning of a prompt. Two prompts share a cache only for as long as their opening bytes are identical, and the moment they diverge, everything after the divergence is billed and processed fresh. Every prompt in this game opened with You are the Foreign Secretary… and put the game transcript underneath — so the shared opening across a turn was the twelve characters "You are the ", and the enormous identical part sat below the point where the prompts had already parted company.

Measured over a ten-turn campaign against a recording endpoint: cacheable prompt text went from 2.1% to 75.8%, and calls sharing their first thousand characters with another call went from 0 of 148 to 80 of 148. On a long saved campaign, the eight big calls in a single turn are 99.4% identical to one another.

06How a model actually gets chosen

One more piece of routing worth seeing, because it affects which model you are really paying for. The game assigns each kind of call a tier — cheap-and-fast or slow-and-capable — and that tier resolves to a Gemini model name. If you are running on OpenRouter, those names mean nothing, so the driver deliberately discards them and uses your configured model instead.

07Two smaller ones, and one in the measuring tools

Moderate

A seeded campaign did not always replay identically

Choosing which international actors respond passed the list through a Python set, whose iteration order for strings varies between runs. Two runs of the same seed could therefore put the same actors in a different order, and a different order means different text. Replaced with an order-preserving de-duplication, which was the intent all along.

Minor

The offline driver had one event, forever

Playing without a key produced the same intelligence report on every turn after the first, because the offline driver returned a single hardcoded event. It now draws from a pool of twelve. This is the behaviour you spotted in the no-key tests.

Minor

The measurement server could write its two logs out of order

What happened. The recording endpoint writes each request to two files — one with the timing record, one with the prompt text — and the analysis pairs them by position. The record was flushed to disk first, so a prompt small enough to sit in the buffer stayed there while its record was already written.

Why it mattered. Only for the tooling, never for players — but the tooling is where every number in this document comes from, so it has to be trustworthy.

The fix, and how it was checked. The prompt is flushed before its record, so "a record on disk implies its prompt is on disk" is now guaranteed. My first two attempts at proving this were worthless: one read the files only after each request finished, which can never observe the gap, and the other used prompts too large to stay buffered. The third — a reader thread racing five request threads with short prompts — showed 40 violations under the old order and 0 under the new one.

08What is not proven

The prefix measurements are exact: they are byte-for-byte comparisons of the prompts the game really emits, made by recording a full campaign. What has not been confirmed is whether the provider honours them. This sandbox cannot reach openrouter.ai, and I never handle your key, so no request has ever been made to the real endpoint from here. Confirming the cost saving needs one live call and a look at the cached-token count on the response.

Cache lifetimes are also short — minutes, not hours. The seven decision-phase calls fire seconds apart and are comfortably inside that. A gap where a player is thinking is not, and the code comments say so rather than overselling it.
